Reactivation Engine — Operating Guide

Tribeca NW · where it lives, how to start it, how to watch it

Where it lives

Four places, and only one of them is new.

PieceWhereExists?
The code github.com/DavidTribeca/reactivation-engine — the source of truth. Nothing runs from here; it's the copy everything else is built from. yes
The running program A Railway service in your compassionate-contentment project, built from that repo. One always-on process that wakes itself on a schedule — no separate cron to set up, nothing on your laptop, nothing you have to remember to run. not yet
The memory Your existing Railway Postgres. New tables, all named re_something, sitting alongside the ISA queue that's already there. Who's due, who's mid-ladder, every attempt, every outcome. migrations not applied
The dashboard A web page the service itself serves, at its Railway address. Appears the moment the service deploys. not yet

Nothing gets installed into Follow Up Boss or GoHighLevel. The engine reaches out to them over the internet, the same way you do when you log in. If you switched the engine off tomorrow, both systems would carry on exactly as they are now.

Why its own Railway service rather than folding into isa-call-list: that service works today and keeps your ISAs supplied. A bad deploy on the new code should never be able to take it down. They share the same database, so there's still one source of truth.

Where the cycle begins

Two things are still outstanding before day one: the three GoHighLevel permissions, and the workflow address. Without the second one the engine has nowhere to drop people, so it cannot dial at all.

Once those land, the first fortnight is deliberately slow. The point is that when something is wrong, it's wrong in front of 100 records rather than 750.

1

Load everyone in. No dialing.

Migrations applied, 23,563 people imported and sorted into waves, suppression list pulled from your do-not-contact pond.

Before moving on: the cohort counts look like the numbers you recognise, and suppression lands near 2,100.

2

Dry run — see exactly who it would call.

It prints the list, in order, and dials nobody.

Before moving on: you personally recognise the names at the top and agree they're the right ones to start with.

3

Break it on purpose.

We deliberately let the suppression list go stale and confirm the engine refuses to dial anyone.

Before moving on: it actually refused. This is the safety mechanism that matters most, and an untested safety mechanism isn't one.

4

First real calls — 100 a day, one group, mornings only.

Your best cohort, mid-morning window, nothing else.

Before moving on: nobody was called outside legal hours, and anyone who opted out landed on the suppression list within the hour.

5

Two weeks of calibration.

Still 100 a day. This is where guesses get replaced by your actual numbers — how many answer, how many are the right person, how many qualify, how many book.

Before moving on: five consecutive green days.

6

The ramp starts.

100 → 125 → 156 → 195, a 25% step per green day, up to 750. Around eight weeks to full speed, roughly six months to work the whole database.

How you watch it — three rhythms

Every day, ten seconds — the status page

Bookmark the Railway address. It refreshes itself every minute, so leaving it in a tab is fine.

One word at the top tells you everything: RUNNING, THROTTLED (it cut volume on its own — usually fine, check the reason), BLOCKED (it stopped itself for safety), or STOPPED (someone hit the emergency stop).

Below that: how many are in flight right now, how many went out today against target, how many are due, progress by cohort, and the last seven days of throttle decisions with the reason for each.

Every Monday, ten minutes — the throttle review

The only recurring thing this program asks of you. Look at the last seven days on the status page and ask one question: is it green?

What you seeWhat it meansWhat you do
greenAnswer rate holding, opt-outs low, your ISAs keeping up.Nothing. It steps volume up 25% by itself.
yellowOne signal drifting. It holds volume flat rather than climbing.Read the reason. Usually resolves on its own.
redSomething is genuinely off. It has already halved volume.Read the reason — it names which of the three signals tripped.

The decision is already coded. The ritual is what makes it real — three red weeks nobody looked at is how a program quietly ends up at the floor for a month.

Whenever you're curious — the burndown

The one number worth watching monthly: estimated months remaining, on the status page. It's recomputed from your actual throughput over the last 14 days, so it corrects the six-month projection with reality instead of assumption. If it starts climbing, something upstream changed.

What the throttle is actually watching

Three signals, each with a minimum sample size so a single bad day can't swing it.

SignalThe question it asks
RolloverOf the conversations the bot handed to a human, how many did nobody get to within 48 hours? This is the capacity question, and it's the one that should govern the ramp — there's no point generating more conversations than your people can work.
Answer rateAre fewer people picking up than they were? A falling answer rate usually means a caller number is getting flagged as spam.
Opt-out rateAre people asking to be left alone more than they were? This is the one that says the script or the targeting is wrong.

When it stops by itself

Every safety mechanism here fails closed — when in doubt it stops rather than carries on. That's correct, and it means the program can stop safely and silently. Three things can halt it:

HaltWhy
Suppression list is staleThe do-not-contact sync hasn't succeeded recently, so the engine's picture of who asked not to be called is out of date. It would rather dial nobody than dial one of them.
Too many in flightToo many people are waiting on an outcome. Usually means SimpleTalk slowed down. It pauses intake and resumes on its own once things drain — this one needs nothing from you.
Emergency stop is onSomeone ran the stop command. The page shows the reason and who did it.

This is exactly why the Slack alert matters more than it sounds. A health check runs three times a day and posts when a human is genuinely needed — stale suppression, no dials on a working day, push failures climbing, outcomes not coming back, three red days running, caller numbers getting flagged, opt-outs above 2%. Without it, a silent halt looks identical to a quiet day.

The stop button

If you ever need every call to stop immediately — a complaint, a lawyer's phone call, a script that's saying the wrong thing:

node scripts/run-dispatch.js stop "reason goes here"

It takes effect on the very next run. No deploy, no code change, no waiting on me. The reason and your name are recorded so whoever finds it stopped knows why. Resume with resume.

Belt and braces: the stop ai call tag in Follow Up Boss still works exactly as it does today, for pulling one individual person out. The stop command is for stopping everyone at once.

Seven scheduled jobs and no daily human step. Once it's running, the program selects, dials, retries, suppresses, tags, throttles and advances waves on its own. Your Monday ten minutes is the whole operating cost.
